# Gooding family research

Research snapshot: 6 October 2026. Initial research baseline for GenealogyGooding.

## Purpose and scope

Build an accurate, documented account of Emily Alice Gooding’s paternal Gooding ancestry, particularly her Black ancestry, potentially as an illustrated keepsake. William (Bill) Larman is Emily’s maternal grandfather. The Genealogy search application is a separate project.

Use documents only: no DNA testing and no approaches to family members. Keep documented evidence, exported tree assertions, family recollections, hypotheses and unresolved questions separate. Earlier Claude research is not authoritative and must be independently checked. No Caribbean island, immigration date, military service or religious affiliation is established merely by a name or family story.

## Family recollections and hypotheses

- Family recollection: John Wesley was Black and lived in the Barrow-in-Furness area.
- Local recollection: people in a Lake District pub remembered him as “the first black man in the Lake District”. The pub, speakers, date and basis of that claim are not recorded here. It is not a proven historical first.
- Family hypothesis: the Gooding surname came from a Caribbean slave owner and the family descends from that owner and an enslaved woman. Surname continuity alone establishes neither ownership nor biological ancestry. No particular owner or enslaved ancestor has been identified in the evidence reviewed.

## Research sequence

1. Inspect the original Ancestry death-index page linked in the source register. Recover the full registration reference and verify the indexed birth date, name, registration month/year and district.
2. Inspect the original marriage-index page for John Wesley; confirm the spouse surname and recover quarter, district, volume and page. Locate Winifred’s corresponding entry. Start with January–March 1952, widening the range if needed.
3. Obtain the death certificate once the match/reference is secure. Transcribe its recorded birth date and birthplace, occupation, address, death details and informant; distinguish reported information from independently corroborated facts.
4. Obtain the marriage certificate. Transcribe names, ages, marital conditions, occupations, addresses, fathers’ names/occupations, witnesses and venue. A father named on a certificate is documentary evidence of the statement, not automatic proof of biological parentage.
5. Corroborate identity through suitable birth registrations, probate, local newspapers, burial records and local archives. Investigate passenger lists and military records only as supported by concrete leads.
6. Once birthplace is established, identify the relevant Caribbean civil/church/archive records and investigate Clifton/Lorenza, including spelling variants. Only then test the surname story against specific people and records.

## Present limitations

This baseline reviews the GEDCOM’s content, citations and family structure; it does not independently authenticate the underlying index records or certificates. No full death or marriage registration reference was present in the reviewed export text. A public web search did not confirm those entries; that search failure is not evidence of their absence. No certificate was reviewed in this chat. No conclusion is reached about immigration, military service, Methodist affiliation, slave ownership or descent from an owner.

## Maintaining the record

Keep this document as the readable master. Add each new record to Evidence-Register.md with its exact source, transcription, access date and assessment. Preserve conflicts until resolved; record why a conclusion changes. Do not silently replace 1924 with 1925. Store original documents separately with stable filenames and retain their provenance. The full main GEDCOM is shared with other research workspaces and should remain unchanged by this project.
